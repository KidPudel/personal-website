## Two worlds and an ocean

Have you ever wondered what a vibe actually is? That atmosphere you sometimes carry around, which makes the whole world look and feel a little different?

Over time, while working through things on my own, I built a simple mental model of how I understand the world. Here is a quick illustration of it.

The first part is an honest one. I'm part of the external world, like all of us. There's nothing to do about that, and no point pretending we live in separate worlds. We're all here together at this party of life, and I find that kind of comforting.

But each of us also has an inner world. I picture it as an ocean. I took that idea from David Lynch and his book *Catching the Big Fish*, and a little from Rick Rubin's idea of being an antenna that picks up ideas from the universe. That's why I think of our inner worlds as one infinitely big ocean. Each of us has our own part of it, and how much of it we get to explore depends on how open our mind is. In that ocean we catch fish: thoughts and emotions. They don't define us. They're something we catch.

These two worlds are always influencing each other, and we're the gate they flow through. When something happens outside, it changes something inside. When something rises inside, it changes how we see and act on what's outside. Noticing that, and deciding what it all means, is on us.

So where does a vibe fit in this picture? It doesn't feel like a fish. It feels more like the ocean itself changed. Like the current started moving differently.

## Three levels of processing

Don Norman, in *Emotional Design*, gives a very simplified model of how our reactions form. He splits brain processing into three levels. The first two work below awareness. The third one is conscious.

The first is **visceral**. It's the oldest, most primitive level, shaped by evolution. It's fast and fire-and-forget, and it works straight through the body. Something feels unsafe, and we tense up. Something feels pleasant, and we relax. Sweetness feels good and bitterness feels wrong, because for most of our history that kept us alive.

The second is **behavioral**. It runs our learned actions, the things we've done so many times we don't think about them anymore. It sets an expectation of what should happen, drives the action, and checks whether the result matched. A match makes us feel in control and satisfied. A mismatch makes us feel frustrated and out of control. But it doesn't ask what the result means, or why it happened.

That's the job of the third level, **reflective**. Reflective processing searches for meaning, looks for causes, and draws conclusions about the past and the future. That's why the emotions it produces stay with us the longest: pride, shame, guilt, nostalgia. They matter to us, so they linger.

These levels also influence each other. A strong visceral reaction can drown out what we consciously think. And conscious thought can calm a visceral reaction or override a habit.

## Which levels make a vibe

Maybe this is where we find our answer. Which of these levels does a vibe actually need?

Behavioral is about action, and you can get into a vibe without doing anything at all. Lying in bed and listening to music is enough. So we're left with two: visceral and reflective.

Let's see how they build an atmosphere. Imagine you're a teenager again. You have a crush on someone. You dream about going to college abroad, somewhere in the West. I did. It's late at night, and you're lying in bed with *Sweater Weather* by The Neighbourhood playing. You've just watched the first *Twilight* and played *Life is Strange*. And you're in this atmosphere of teenage romance at an American high school, somewhere rainy in Washington.

You're probably not even thinking about it. You're just sensing it. You live somewhere beyond the pines. You know the café where they give you black coffee for free. You go to the beach with your friends on a rainy day. It's all in your head, or in your head and your body.

That sounds like visceral processing meeting reflective processing. But visceral reactions are fire-and-forget. They don't last. A vibe does. It lingers, and lingering is what reflective processing does.

## The loop

So here's what I think a vibe is made of. Visceral processing of what's outside: sound, rhythm, color, light. Visceral processing of what's inside: our own taste. And reflective processing of memories, and of how all of it connects to ourselves. That last part binds everything together and changes the visceral feeling even further.

Then that feeling, a physical one, starts working like a lens. We see everything around us through it, so it changes the information coming back to our mind. We reflect on that changed information, it shapes our thoughts, and our thoughts feed the feeling again.

That's the loop, and that's how a visceral feeling gets to linger. A vibe is a reflective state that manifests in visceral experience. The loop keeps it alive, and we see the world through it.

Back in the ocean, it isn't a fish we caught. It's the way the current runs. And that brings certain kinds of fish to us.

I think designers work with this too, mostly without noticing. Products have atmospheres, made of the same things: color, light, sound, pace. If one song can tint a whole evening, an app we open every day can tint something as well.
