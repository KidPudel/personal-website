## What I missed

In [*Identity Cage*](/writing/identity-cage/) I offered a simple idea: identity is a self-limitation, and once you recognize it, you can just let go of it. I treated it as a problem in thinking.

But I've seen it happen many times, and not only in my own circle. I'd talk with people about ideas that touched who they are, and watch their whole posture change. Seemingly reasonable people started to feel threatened. And they defended. Sometimes by finding even stronger reasons to stay inside. Sometimes by attacking.

There's a name for this: identity-protective cognition. We don't only ask whether something is true. We also ask, without noticing, what it would cost us to believe it. And when the cost is our place among our own people, the answer is usually decided before the thinking starts.

## It's warm in there

Nobody stays in a cage for no reason. It's warm in there. Everyone you know is inside with you, and being inside means you belong.

It also explains things. If something hurt you, the cage tells you exactly who to blame. Real pain is messy. It has many causes. Most of them have no face, and the ones that do are often out of reach. So the blame goes where it's easier to put: on someone close enough to hate. The cage tells you who that is.

And hatred brings its own kind of freedom. You don't have to understand the other side anymore. You don't have to wonder whether you're part of the problem. You don't have to feel bad about how you treat them, because they deserve it. That's a lot of weight taken off at once.

So when someone offers an argument, they're asking you to give all of that up. Belonging, a clear story, a light conscience. And the argument offers nothing back except being right.

## Smarter walls

This is where intelligence comes in, and not the way I expected.

If the cage is a safe space, intelligence doesn't open the door. It reinforces the walls. The more intelligence you add, the better you are at finding reasons, answering objections and spotting the weak point in the other person's argument. Each reason is a new brick.

That's why arguing with a smart person inside a cage can be harder, not easier. You're not arguing with their identity. You're arguing with its lawyer, and a good one.

## Force without direction

I think of intelligence as a force. It decides how hard you can push. But it doesn't decide where. The direction comes from somewhere else: from what you already want to be true, from what you're afraid to lose.

So intelligence is not the same thing as an open mind. Keith Stanovich spent years studying this. Smart people are just as likely as anyone to judge evidence in favor of their own side. They're only better at doing it. And when the direction is set by fear or belonging, a strong force just takes you further the wrong way. Intelligence can be blind.

It's a joke, but it's also the trap. If who I am rests on being smart, then being wrong doesn't just hurt my opinion. It hurts me. And then my intelligence becomes another cage, and the one guarding it.

What intelligence lacks is the ability to see its own direction. That's what I mean by awareness.

By awareness I don't mean knowing about biases. People who know about biases mostly spot them in other people. Awareness is noticing it in yourself. The small flinch when someone says you might be wrong, before any arguments arrive. That flinch is the direction being set.

It doesn't make the bias disappear. The flinch still comes. In [*What is a vibe?*](/writing/what-is-a-vibe/) I wrote that thoughts and emotions are fish we catch, and they don't define us. The flinch is one of those fish. Once you see it as a fish, it loses some of its weight. It's still there, but it no longer decides for you.

## What I added myself

I still catch these fish all the time. Here's one, over something very small.

One evening my cat was lying next to my girlfriend. I walked into the room, and she jumped up and ran off to the sofa. And I got offended. Not a little. It felt like there was a family of three, and I'd just been voted out of it.

I knew it was silly while I was feeling it. It's just a cat. But knowing that didn't make the feeling stop. Part of me didn't want to let it go.

What helped was not fighting the feeling. I let it be there, not hiding, not holding. It's okay to feel offended, even by a cat. And once I let it be, its weight dropped.

Then I could see what actually happened. I walked in, and she ran to the sofa. She wasn't running from me. She wanted me to come and play. The rejection, the not belonging, I added all of that myself.

## Same logic, different scale

In the first part I wrote that a backend engineer is not a kind of person. It's a name for a set of capabilities. At that scale, it's easy to see and easy to agree with. Almost nobody's life depends on it.

Now take exactly the same logic and apply it to a country. A person from somewhere is not a kind of person either. They didn't choose where they were born, and they're not responsible for what others did there. It's the same mistake, just bigger.

But at this scale, something strange happens. People who would agree with the first example start to struggle with the second. Not because the logic got harder. The words start to move. "Equal" gets a "but" attached to it. "Responsible" starts to mean "from the same place". "It's different here" appears, without ever saying how.

Definitions shuffle until the conclusion is safe again. The bigger the identity, the more there is to protect, and the more the meaning of words will bend to protect it.

## The wrong channel

At some point I realized I was trying to fix the problem through the one channel it doesn't come through.

Identity beliefs don't change through argument. They change when life gives someone a different experience. Usually it's meeting real people from the other side, over time, as equals. Not hearing about them. Being with them.

And I can't give that to anyone. I'm not running their life. To really change one person, you'd have to live for it, and even then it might not work.

So after a while I had to admit that the effort often isn't worth it. Not because those people don't matter. Because arguing was never going to reach them.

## What's left

Accepting a person can't mean deciding they're fixed, that this is just what they are. That would be the same mistake, just pointed the other way. So I try to accept where they are, not what they are.

Arguing doesn't open minds. It gives people something to defend. A life lived by what you believe asks nothing of them, so there's nothing to push back against. That's the part I can do: stay aware, keep my own mind open, and live what I believe.

I don't have to prove anything. Some people will see it when they're ready. Some won't, and that's okay too.
