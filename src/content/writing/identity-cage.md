We all try to fit in. We seem wired to seek belonging because, for most of human history, belonging to a group was necessary for survival.

We are often so eager to adopt a label and gain a sense of identity that we do not stop to question whether we still need to treat those labels so seriously.

Today, we all want to be unique, yet we still try to belong to *the best possible group that fits us*, searching for the best possible one instead of asking ourselves what we actually need. This resembles the distinction between a [maximizer and a satisficer](https://alicegao.substack.com/p/3-are-you-a-maximizer-or-a-satisficer).

Now that most of us possess at least some general sense of human community and understand that we are all *Earthlings*, not aliens to one another, why do we still feel the need to divide ourselves further? Maybe that is already enough.

But this topic is much broader than this post. So let us focus on specific activities and look at how we think and behave there. And, most importantly, what *identity* does to us.

*By identity, I mean a label treated not merely as a description, but as a dogmatic boundary around what someone is allowed to do or to be.*

## Identity in life’s equation

For this section, the spotlight will mainly be on software engineering because that is where I have the most experience.

> “Ew, I won’t touch JavaScript. It’s embarrassing,” said a person with a Rust-developer identity.

*They went to build their own browser, abandoned it after one evening, and ended up doing nothing.*

> “Wait, you’re using VS Code? DOn’T yOu KnOw… it’s Microsoft? It’s an evil corporation. Neovim is obviously the best option, or you’re not a real developer,” said some terminal-only individual.

*Later that day, they spent another five and a half hours choosing between Catppuccin Mocha and Tokyo Night.*

> “If you’re not using AI, you’re going to be left behind,” said some average Andy.

*After making this comment, they spent another four hours refreshing X.*

> “AI is for vibe coders,” said no-AI Andy from the opposite camp.

*Later that day, he repeated the same opinion to four colleagues and spent another two and a half hours fixing a Gradle dependency-version conflict.*

*You get the point.*

- “Clean code requires this specific architecture.”
- “Monoliths are for amateurs.”
- “Frontend developers are not real engineers.”
- “Console players are casuals.”
- “A parent who needs daycare is letting someone else raise their child.”
- “You’re not a real fan if you only know the popular albums.”
- “Real musicians play instruments.”

These are stereotypes and deliberately exaggerated examples, but they are all around us. You have probably encountered some of them before.

They are funny from the outside. But the same mechanism becomes painful when those rules are directed inward.

A narrow identity does not merely make someone annoying to other people. From my own experience, it can also become a source of anxiety, insecurity, and unnecessary self-imposed limitations.

It encourages black-and-white thinking instead of [gray thinking](https://fs.blog/value-grey-thinking/).

It makes people afraid to expose themselves to the richness of the world and its possibilities.

“I’m a backend engineer. Game development is for game developers. I’ll look unserious, waste time, and fall behind in my real field.”

Even though nothing is actually stopping them, crossing the boundary feels forbidden. It becomes a fear of doing something outside what they are *allowed* to do or be.

Identity can also create an existential threat whenever you miss something inside it.

“A new nightly version of Crap.js was released. If I don’t master it immediately, I’ll stop being relevant. Maybe I’m not really the kind of engineer I thought I was.”

Missing anything now feels dangerous because you have isolated yourself from everything outside that identity.

So, if a narrow identity puts someone inside a cage, what should we do?

## Narrow versus wide

What if the identity became wider?

> “I’m not a backend developer, frontend developer, mobile developer, game developer, painter, or songwriter. I’m a creator.”

Once the identity is wide, nothing stops someone from trying anything they want.

Right?

It is not that simple.

A narrow identity makes exploration feel dangerous.

A wide identity can make commitment feel dangerous.

Instead of being afraid to try something new, you become afraid to focus on one thing more than the others. Specializing starts to feel like abandoning the identity of a generalist.

This happened in my own life.

I moved from a narrow professional identity toward seeing myself as a creator. It initially felt freeing. Eventually, however, I caught myself being unable to commit to one thing for long.

> “Wait, I don’t want to do only this. I’m a creator, not a backend engineer.”

I felt empty and overwhelmed. There were too many things to do, too many possibilities to explore, and no clear reason to choose one over another.

The identity had become wider, but it was still an identity.

I had crashed headfirst into another cage.

It also creates another practical problem.

In professional life, never specializing in anything makes it harder for other people to understand how you can help them.

Someone needs a particular problem solved.

How are they supposed to know that you possess the capabilities they need?

We therefore still need a way to communicate what we can reliably do.

But it does not have to define who we are.

## Identity in its proper place

Before returning to identity, let us first break down what work actually is.

Doing so reveals where identity does, and does not, belong.

Someone wants something to be created, changed, maintained, restored, prevented, or made possible.

You apply a set of capabilities toward that outcome.

As a result, the task is completed, and a desired capability, behaviour, experience, or result now exists in the world.

It sounds bare-bones, but that is intentional. It strips work down to its fundamental pieces without the cultural weight attached to it.

And the result can be anything:

- a software tool;
- a better physique for someone you trained;
- a child who learned to read;
- a painting you gave your mother for her birthday;
- a love letter you wrote for your wife.

Once we crack work open, there is no identity inside the mechanism itself.

There is only:

> **Capabilities applied successfully → a desired outcome becomes real.**

Nothing about possessing one capability prevents you from developing another.

You can focus on what you care about, explore the world, try new things, and go as deep as you want.

Identity does not need to disappear. It only needs to stop governing what you are allowed to do.

But we still need a way to communicate what we can do.

Look again at what we have:

A task requires a particular set of capabilities.

That is all.

And we already have a couple of words for communicating such capability sets:

- **Role**: a convenient name for an *approximate* package of capabilities and responsibilities.
- **Title**: a lossy label used to communicate a conventional role.

A backend engineer is not a kind of person.

It is a convenient name for someone who possesses a particular collection of capabilities.

A game developer is not a separate species.

It is another package of capabilities, many of which may overlap with backend, frontend, design, art, data, or other roles.

Roles and titles are useful when they help other people understand what we can currently do.

They become cages when they begin deciding what we are allowed to do next.

A title can describe the capabilities you currently offer without defining the limits of who you can become.

You can deepen a capability without turning it into your identity.

You can specialize without surrendering your freedom.

You can commit without imprisoning yourself.

You can use a title without allowing the title to use you.
